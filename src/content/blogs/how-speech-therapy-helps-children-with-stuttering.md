If your child has started repeating sounds, getting stuck on words or struggling to push sentences out, you already know the mix of worry and helplessness that comes with it. Stuttering in children is more common than most parents realise and, with the right support started early, most children go on to speak fluently.

## Understanding What Stuttering Actually Is

Stuttering is a speech fluency disorder characterised by involuntary repetitions of sounds or syllables, prolongations of sounds, or complete blocks where speech stops temporarily. It is not a sign of low intelligence. It is not caused by anxiety, poor parenting or a lack of confidence. Research using brain imaging has consistently shown that stuttering has neurological roots, with differences in the speech motor processing pathways of people who stutter compared to fluent speakers. Genetics also play a significant role.

Approximately 5% of all children stutter at some point during development, with boys affected three to four times more often than girls. Most cases emerge between the ages of 2 and 5, during the most rapid phase of language development.

## Why Early Intervention Changes Everything

The single most important factor in outcomes for children who stutter is when therapy begins. Research consistently shows that around 80% of children who receive speech therapy before the age of 7 achieve normal or near-normal fluency. After this window, progress is still absolutely possible but requires more sustained effort over a longer period.

## How Speech Therapy Works

There are two primary evidence-based approaches used by qualified speech-language pathologists.

Fluency shaping therapy teaches children to produce speech with easy, relaxed airflow and gentle voice onset. The technique is practiced gradually from single words through to full conversation and is especially effective with younger children.

Stuttering modification therapy takes a different approach. Rather than eliminating stutters, it teaches children to stutter more easily and openly, reducing the fear and avoidance that often accompany the condition and cause it to worsen over time.

Both approaches are typically combined with desensitisation work, which builds emotional resilience by reducing shame and anxiety around speaking. For younger children, parent-child interaction therapy involves coaching parents to create low-pressure speaking environments at home, where the child feels genuinely heard.

## What You Can Do as a Parent Today

Give your child time to finish their thought without interrupting or completing their sentences. Maintain warm, natural eye contact while they speak. Model slightly slower speech yourself, as children naturally mirror their parents' rate. Avoid telling them to slow down, relax or think before they speak. These well-meaning instructions shift focus to the stuttering rather than the message and can increase self-consciousness significantly.
